Cutting AI cost per question by 90% while usage tripled

We were burning through around $300 of AI credits a day, and the bill grew with every new user. Growth explained the total. It didn’t explain why each question cost so much, and fixing that meant rethinking how the product’s AI was built.

Ownercost analysis, the case for the rebuild, launch testing

The Problem

SuperInvesting answers investing questions about 5,000+ Indian stocks with AI. Every question costs money in model calls, so AI cost is one of our biggest variable costs, and it grows with every user we add.

We were spending around $300 of AI credits a day. Usage was growing fast, so a rising bill was expected, and the total moved in line with users and questions. But a bill that grows with usage is only fine if each question is cheap, and ours wasn’t.

Where the Money Went

I own AI cost analysis, so I broke the spend down instead of reading the total: how many questions we answered, how many model calls each one needed, and what each call cost. The calls per question were the problem.

Our chat ran on an orchestrator and 7 agents, each one its own LLM with its own custom prompt. For each question the orchestrator picked the agents it needed and usually called several in parallel. Each agent wrote a response, they all went back to the orchestrator, and only then did it write the answer the user saw. One question could mean several LLM calls, with one model writing an answer from other models’ answers.

When a cost moves, split it into volume, intensity and price before deciding what the story is.

The Options

  1. Absorb it. Keep the architecture and accept higher cost per user, which gets worse with growth.
  2. Swap the model only. Quick, but the multiplier stays for the next price change.
  3. Rebuild around one model with tools. One model decides what to do and calls a stock screener, live search or research tool only when it needs them, so it works from raw data instead of other models’ opinions. More work, but it removes the multiplier.

The Decision

I made the case for option 3 first, because it was the main lever on cost, then for a model switch on top of it. Doing it in that order meant we didn’t just swap one expensive setup for a cheaper version of the same problem.

Building It

Vaibhav Kumar Singh built the new system and executed it flawlessly. The hardest open question was the new model itself: whether it could use tools, and how well it could use all of them. It could. Along the way the chat got things it never had: a sources button, citations for every news item, and a PDF viewer so official filings and annual reports open in a sidebar inside the app.

Shipping It Safely

A few people on the team were worried. The predicted drop in cost was so large that they assumed we had to be losing quality somewhere, and in investing a wrong number costs users real money. I ran the testing on staging and locally, eval after eval, and the quality held every time.

The Result

  • Cost per 1,000 questions fell from $48 to $5, about 90% lower.
  • Over the same period, monthly AI questions grew from 29K to 90K and paid users from 5.4K to 12.8K, driven by the Economic Times partnership.
  • The chat got better too: sources, citations and filings that open in the app.
  • Over a year, the savings are money we can now put into growing the company and the platform.

What I Took Away

For AI products, cost is a product decision, not just an engineering one. The architecture decides what every question costs, and that decides which growth you can afford. The engineering background helped me see the multiplier. Product made it my job to argue for removing it.
